What you can do here. Read why reverse osmosis (RO) dominates desalination (§2) and the physics behind it (§3). Then change the feed, the membrane and the operating pressure of one pressure vessel and watch the flux, salt concentrations and pressures change along it (§4). See the energy it uses compared with the thermodynamic minimum, and what the water costs (§5).

How it works. The page loads Pyodide, a version of Python compiled to WebAssembly, then fetches ro_model.py from this site and runs it on your device. No server does any computation. The same file is tested on every change by the automated tests, so the model you use is exactly the model that was checked.

2. Why reverse osmosis matters

A 2019 global survey counted 15,906 operational desalination plants producing about 95 million m³ of fresh water per day, with 48 % of it made in the Middle East and North Africa [1]. The same plants discharge about 142 million m³/day of brine [1], the concentrated salt stream that is the unavoidable by-product of desalination (and that our model computes).

Membrane processes, almost all of them reverse osmosis, now produce about 69 % of global desalination capacity [2], having overtaken thermal distillation. The reason is energy: thermal plants boil water, while RO only has to push it through a membrane against the osmotic pressure.

Energy: the number that decides everything

Separating fresh water from 35 g/L seawater at 50 % recovery needs at least about 1.06 kWh per m³ of fresh water, a limit set by thermodynamics, not by technology [7] TODO-VERIFY. Real plants use more, partly because a real stage must push harder than the minimum (see the pinch in §3). Most of the pressure energy in the brine is recovered with isobaric pressure exchangers, which transfer about 95–97 % of it to the incoming feed [8]. At Carlsbad these devices cut the energy use of the RO process by 46 %, saving an estimated 146 million kWh per year [6].

Who makes the membranes

Nearly all RO membranes are thin-film composite polyamide films rolled into spiral-wound elements, typically 8 inches across and 40 inches long with about 440 ft² (40.9 m²) of membrane [9]. Major suppliers include DuPont (FilmTec) [9], Toray, which supplied the Taweelah membranes [10], Nitto (Hydranautics) [11] and LG Chem [12].

Where RO is used

  • Seawater desalination (SWRO): about 35 g/L feed [16], high pressure (the default case here).
  • Brackish water (BWRO): groundwater or estuaries with a few g/L of salt; much lower pressure and higher recovery (try the Brackish preset).
  • Potable reuse: purifying treated wastewater to drinking-water quality.
  • Ultrapure water: semiconductor fabs, power plants and pharmaceuticals, usually RO followed by further polishing.

The physics

3. Theory

3.1 Osmotic pressure

Salt water has a lower chemical potential of water than pure water. Separated by a membrane that passes only water, pure water flows into the salt side until the salt side is at a higher pressure, the osmotic pressure \(\pi\). Reverse osmosis applies a pressure above \(\pi\) to drive water the other way. For dilute solutions the van’t Hoff equation gives

\[ \pi = i\,\frac{C}{M}\,R\,T \]

with \(i = 2\) ions per NaCl, \(C\) the salt concentration in g/L (= kg/m³) and \(M = 58.44\) g/mol. For 35 g/L at 25 °C this gives 29.7 bar. Real salt solutions are not ideal; the osmotic coefficient \(\varphi\) corrects this, \(\pi = \varphi\,\pi_{\text{van’t Hoff}}\). We use the Pitzer model for NaCl at 25 °C [13] TODO-VERIFY:

\[ \varphi - 1 = -A_\varphi\frac{\sqrt{m}}{1 + 1.2\sqrt{m}} + m\left(\beta^{(0)} + \beta^{(1)} e^{-2\sqrt{m}}\right) + m^2 C^{\varphi} \]

with \(m\) the molality, \(A_\varphi = 0.392\), \(\beta^{(0)} = 0.0765\), \(\beta^{(1)} = 0.2664\) and \(C^\varphi = 0.00127\). It reproduces tabulated osmotic coefficients of NaCl to within 0.003 from 0.1 to 3 mol/kg [18] TODO-VERIFY (check V03 in §6). At seawater strength \(\varphi \approx 0.92\), so van’t Hoff overestimates \(\pi\) by about 8 % (27.4 instead of 29.7 bar). The model uses van’t Hoff by default; the Pitzer switch in §4 shows the difference.

3.2 The membrane: solution–diffusion

Water and salt dissolve in the dense polyamide layer and diffuse across it independently. The water flux \(J_w\) is driven by the net driving pressure (NDP); the salt flux \(J_s\) by the concentration difference:

\[ J_w = A\left[\Delta P - (\pi_m - \pi_p)\right],\qquad J_s = B\,(C_m - C_p),\qquad C_p = \frac{J_s}{J_w} \]

\(A\) is the water permeance (L m⁻² h⁻¹ bar⁻¹, “LMH/bar”) and \(B\) the salt permeance (LMH). Seawater membranes trade a low \(A\) for a very low \(B\); brackish membranes are more open [14] TODO-VERIFY. Because salt passage does not depend on pressure but water flux does, higher pressure gives purer water locally. Both permeances rise with temperature by a factor \(\text{TCF} = \exp[K(1/298 - 1/T)]\), with \(K = 2640\) K above 25 °C and 3020 K below [15] TODO-VERIFY.

3.3 Concentration polarisation

Water leaves through the membrane but salt is left behind, so salt piles up in a thin boundary layer. The concentration at the membrane surface \(C_m\) is higher than in the bulk \(C_b\). A film model with mass-transfer coefficient \(k\) gives

\[ \frac{C_m - C_p}{C_b - C_p} = \exp\!\left(\frac{J_w}{k}\right) \]

The ratio \(C_m/C_b\) is the CP factor. It raises \(\pi_m\), which lowers the flux and lets more salt through. Designers keep it below about 1.2 TODO-VERIFY.

How the model solves it. For a given \(J_w\) the three equations above give \(C_p\) and \(C_m\) in closed form:

\[ C_p = \frac{B\,E\,C_b}{J_w + B\,E},\qquad C_m = C_p + (C_b - C_p)\,E,\qquad E = e^{J_w/k} \]

so the local problem reduces to one equation in \(J_w\), solved with a bracketed root finder (Illinois method). A root is guaranteed: at zero flux a leaky membrane rejects nothing, so the residual is negative, and at \(J_w = A\Delta P\) it is positive.

3.4 One pressure vessel

An 8-inch pressure vessel holds several elements in series; we use 7 elements, 7.1 m and 286 m² by default. Feed enters at one end and loses water as it flows; the brine leaves at the other end. Along the vessel coordinate \(x\), with \(W\) the membrane area per metre:

\[ \frac{dQ}{dx} = -J_w W,\qquad \frac{d(Q\,C_b)}{dx} = -J_w C_p W,\qquad \frac{dP}{dx} = -\frac{\Delta P_{\text{drop}}}{L} \]

At every point the local membrane problem gives \(J_w\) and \(C_p\). The equations are integrated with the 4th-order Runge–Kutta method; the number of steps is doubled until the results stop changing (relative change below 10⁻⁴). Recovery is \(r = Q_p/Q_f\), the fraction of the feed that becomes fresh water.

3.5 The pinch: why recovery is limited

As water leaves, the brine gets saltier and its osmotic pressure rises. For an ideal membrane without polarisation, the brine at recovery \(r\) has \(\pi_b = \pi_f/(1 - r)\). Flux stops when \(\pi_b\) reaches the applied pressure, giving the single-stage recovery limit

\[ r_{\max} = 1 - \frac{\pi_f}{\Delta P} \]

For seawater at 60 bar, \(r_{\max} \approx 50\,\%\). This is the RO version of a pinch in McCabe–Thiele analysis: there, an operating line that touches the equilibrium curve needs infinitely many stages; here, a pressure line that touches the rising brine osmotic-pressure curve needs infinite membrane area. Membrane added beyond the pinch produces almost nothing. Plot 3 in §4 shows the gap between \(P\) and \(\pi\) closing, and plot 6 shows recovery approaching \(r_{\max}\) as pressure changes.

Two consequences that the model shows:

  • With a leaky membrane (\(B > 0\)) the flux never quite stops. Instead the rejection collapses as the NDP approaches zero: the little water that still passes carries almost as much salt as the feed. Because some salt leaves with the permeate, the brine is slightly less concentrated than for an ideal membrane, so recovery can edge just past \(r_{\max}\), which is defined for an ideal membrane.
  • At the vessel level, rejection is not always improved by more pressure. At the default 9 m³/h feed it peaks near 70 bar: beyond that, extra recovery concentrates the brine so much that the permeate made near the outlet is saltier. (The original plan expected rejection to rise steadily; the tests found otherwise.)

3.6 Energy

The high-pressure pump lifts all the feed to \(P_f\), but only the fraction \(r\) becomes product, so per m³ of permeate (1 bar on 1 m³ = 1/36 kWh):

\[ \text{SEC}_{\text{no ERD}} = \frac{P_f}{\eta_p\,r},\qquad \text{SEC}_{\text{ERD}} = \frac{P_f - \eta_{\text{ERD}}\,(1-r)\,P_b}{\eta_p\,r} \]

The second form includes an energy-recovery device that returns a fraction \(\eta_{\text{ERD}}\) of the brine’s pressure energy to the feed. The thermodynamic minimum for removing pure water reversibly is the average osmotic pressure seen while the brine concentrates:

\[ \text{SEC}_{\min} = \frac{1}{r}\int_0^r \pi\!\left(\frac{C_f}{1-r'}\right)dr' \;\overset{\text{van’t Hoff}}{=}\; \frac{\pi_f}{r}\ln\frac{1}{1-r} \]

A real membrane also lets salt through, which makes the separation easier, so we also compute the minimum work for the separation it actually makes (permeate \(C_p\), brine \(C_b\)), from a free-energy density \(g(c)\) consistent with \(\pi(c)\):

\[ \text{SEC}_{\min,\text{actual}} = \frac{r\,g(C_p) + (1-r)\,g(C_b) - g(C_f)}{r},\qquad \pi = c\,g'(c) - g(c) \]

The second law requires SEC ≥ SECmin,actual; the tests check this for hundreds of random cases. A single stage also needs at least the brine osmotic pressure at the outlet, \(\pi_f/(1-r)\), which is why real SWRO is well above the minimum.

3.7 What the model leaves out

  • Salt is NaCl only. Real seawater has many ions; its osmotic pressure is close to that of 35 g/L NaCl but not identical (seawater salinity is about 35 g/kg [16]).
  • \(A\), \(B\) and \(k\) are constant along the vessel. In reality \(k\) falls as the cross-flow slows, and membranes compact under pressure.
  • The vessel is one 1D channel: spacers, permeate-side pressure and channel geometry are not modelled. Element datasheets are used only for orders of magnitude.
  • Fouling and scaling are not modelled. In real plants they control the design: particles, organics and biofilms foul the membrane, and as brine concentrates, sparingly soluble salts (calcium carbonate, calcium sulfate) can precipitate on it. This is why plants need pretreatment and antiscalants, and why recovery is often limited below the pinch. The model warns when the brine gets very concentrated.
  • The van’t Hoff and Pitzer models estimate molality from g/L and ignore the temperature dependence of the Pitzer parameters.

What it costs

5. Economics

The vessel results are scaled to a whole plant by counting vessels: \(n_{\text{vessels}} = \lceil Q_{\text{plant}} / (24\,Q_{p,\text{vessel}}) \rceil\). Capital cost is the membranes, pressure vessels and high-pressure pumps with energy recovery, multiplied by an “other plant” factor for the intake, pretreatment, outfall, buildings and engineering. It is annualised with the capital recovery factor

\[ \text{CRF} = \frac{i\,(1+i)^n}{(1+i)^n - 1} \]

and added to electricity (SEC × price), membrane replacement and fixed operation and maintenance. This is a teaching model, not a cost estimate. The default unit costs are illustrative TODO-VERIFY; change them to see what drives the price. For comparison, large SWRO plants are reported to produce water for roughly $0.50–1.00 per m³, depending on site, energy price and financing [17]. The SEC here counts only the RO pumps, not intake, pretreatment or post-treatment, so it underestimates the plant total.

What the tests caught

Writing the tests before trusting the model found things the plan got wrong. Three examples from the decisions log:

  • The plan expected observed rejection to rise steadily with feed pressure. The vessel model showed a maximum near 70 bar (§3.5); the explanation was kept as a teaching point.
  • The plan’s root-finder tolerance (10⁻¹² m/s) was too loose to show the 4th-order convergence of the Runge–Kutta method; it was tightened to 10⁻¹⁶ m/s.
  • A test of the recovery limit with 1000× the membrane area failed because the pinch happened within millimetres, which the grid could not resolve. With 10× the area, the model matches \(r_{\max}\) to better than 10⁻⁶.
